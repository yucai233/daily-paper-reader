# Efficient Neural Field Learning via Adaptive Coverage and Focused Sampling 

Guang Zhao Xihaier Luo Huan-Hsin Tseng Seungjun Lee Shinjae Yoo Yihui Ren Wei Xu 

Computing and Data Sciences, Brookhaven National Laboratory Upton, NY 11973, USA https://www.bnl.gov/compsci/ gzhao@bnl.gov, xluo@bnl.gov, htseng@bnl.gov, slee19@bnl.gov sjyoo@bnl.gov, yren@bnl.gov, xuw@bnl.gov 

Abstract 

Implicit neural representations (INRs) provide a flexible framework for modeling high-dimensional continuous fields, but their training is often inefficient due to uniform subsampling that ignores spatial heterogeneity. Existing adaptive sampling methods partially address this issue by prioritizing high-error samples, but typically operate at the point level, often leading to redundant sampling in localized regions and insufficient coverage of the domain. We propose 

ACES (Adaptive Coverage-aware Efficient Sampling) , a structured sampling framework that improves training efficiency by decoupling coverage and importance. ACES constructs adaptive spatial partitions to ensure domain coverage and reduce redundancy, and applies region-level importance weighting to prioritize informative regions during training. We provide a theoretical analysis showing that adaptive partitioning reduces gradient variance by increasing within-region homogeneity, and that controlled bias in region-level weighting may improve optimization efficiency relative to standard unbiased estimators. Experiments on scientific field learning tasks demonstrate that ACES achieves faster convergence and lower error than uniform and pointwise adaptive sampling baselines, with the largest gains in fields with highly localized complexity. 

# 1 Introduction 

Modern scientific discovery increasingly relies on accurate, efficient, and flexible representations of high-dimensional continuous fields, such as spatiotemporal physical states, material microstructures, and environmental transport processes (Park et al., 2025; Qu et al., 2026). Many of these systems are naturally defined on continuous, irregular, or evolving domains, where measurements and simulations provide large collections of pointwise evaluations rather than a single fixed grid. This has motivated implicit neural representations (INRs), a growing class of machine learning methods that optimize pointwise objectives over continuous spaces (Sitzmann et al., 2020; Mildenhall et al., 2020; Park et al., 2019). These methods are flexible and resolution-independent, naturally handle complex geometry, remain compatible with gradient-based optimization, and are well suited to surrogate modeling, inverse problems, and accelerating simulation-driven science. Despite their conceptual elegance, INRs often suffer from significant training inefficiency when learning continuous fields. In many practical settings, millions to billions of candidate points are available from simulations, sensors, or trajectory evaluations of the field, yet computational and memory constraints limit how many can be used per iteration. Uniform sampling fails to account for this heterogeneity, allocating excessive computation to simple regions while under-resolving the complex regions that dominate approximation error. This observation naturally motivates adaptive sampling, where training points are selected or reweighted to prioritize informative regions and improve efficiency. A range of methods have been proposed along this direction, typically using local criteria such as prediction error, residual magnitude, or gradient norm to guide sampling (Zhang et al., 2024; Kheradmand et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025; Tack et al., 2023). While these approaches can accelerate convergence in simple settings, they remain largely heuristic and exhibit two fundamental limitations. First, most treat samples independently and ignore the spatiotemporal structure of the loss field, often causing over-concentration in localized regions where nearby points provide redundant training signals, at the expense of global coverage. Second, while many methods implicitly introduce biased gradient estimators through non-uniform sampling (Zhang et al., 2024; Kheradmand et al., 2024), this bias is neither explicitly analyzed nor systematically exploited. In practice, such bias often accelerates convergence, yet existing work lacks a principled understanding of why this occurs or how to design it effectively. To address these issues, we propose ACES (Adaptive Coverage-aware Efficient Sampling) ,a structured adaptive sampling framework that explicitly accounts for the heterogeneity of the loss field. ACES combines structure-aware domain partitioning with bias-controlled sampling. First, ACES shifts from point-level to region-level sampling: it adaptively partitions the domain according to the evolving loss structure, enabling sampling decisions that respect spatial organization rather than purely local signals. This partitioning enforces coverage while reducing redundancy, preventing excessive clustering. Second, within each adaptive region, ACES further incorporates a bias-controlled importance weighting mechanism to prioritize informative samples. We provide theoretical insights into the role of bias in gradient estimation, suggesting how controlled bias may improve optimization efficiency, and use this analysis to guide the design of our estimator. Together, these components yield a sampling strategy that balances coverage, variance reduction, and fast convergence for learning high-dimensional continuous fields. Adaptive sampling for implicit neural representations. Given the continuous coordinate-based nature of INRs, prior work has explored adaptive sampling for INR training by prioritizing samples with high residuals, uncertainty, or other local importance signals (Zhang et al., 2024, 2025; Kheradmand et al., 2024; Tack et al., 2023). These approaches primarily operate at the individual point level and largely ignore higher-level spatial structure. ACES differs by using adaptive partitioning and region-level weighting to better balance coverage and focus. 

Variance reduction through structured mini-batches. Variance reduction in stochastic optimization has been widely studied through importance sampling and structured mini-batch con-struction. Importance-sampling methods prioritize informative samples using smoothness, gradient norm, or loss-based criteria (Needell et al., 2014; Katharopoulos and Fleuret, 2018), while stratified sampling reduces variance by partitioning data into more homogeneous groups (Zhao and Zhang, 2014). However, these methods are primarily designed for fixed datasets with independently sampled examples and do not exploit the spatial structure of continuous neural fields. In contrast, ACES adaptively partitions continuous domains to jointly improve coverage and variance reduction. 

Weighted sampling and controlled bias. Beyond unbiased variance reduction, recent studies have shown that optimization performance depends not only on raw gradient variance but also on the relationship between gradient variance and update quality (Faghri et al., 2020). ACES performs adaptive region-level sampling over continuous domains and explicitly studies the role of controlled bias through region-level weighting and gradient-alignment analysis.

ACES Overview As illustrated in Fig. 2, ACES performs adaptive region-level sampling over the continuous domain. It begins with a coarse partition and recursively refines regions that remain highly variable, producing more homogeneous regions in which sampled points better represent their local neighborhoods. Sampling and weighting are then applied at the region level rather than independently per point, improving coverage while reducing redundant sampling in spatially correlated areas. Regions with greater remaining variability receive larger sampling budgets, while samples from regions with poorer predictions are assigned greater weights. ACES uses a two-timescale implementation. The partition is updated periodically on a slow timescale, while region weights are updated every iteration using lightweight residual statistics. This is because the spatial structure of the loss field evolves gradually during training, while the sampling weights directly influence which regions contribute most strongly to the parameter update. This separation amortizes partitioning cost while allowing the sampling distribution to adapt online. The following sections answer two questions: First, how should the domain be partitioned so that stochastic gradient estimates have low variance? Second, how should samples or weights be assigned across regions when the goal is not only unbiased gradient estimation, but also faster optimization? We address these questions through a variance-reduction analysis of adaptive partitioning, followed by a bias–variance analysis of region-level weighted sampling.

# 4 Experiments 

We evaluate ACES on spatial and joint spatiotemporal field reconstruction tasks with heterogeneous structure. Our experiments examine sample efficiency, the trade-off between convergence and wall-clock cost, the complementary roles of adaptive partitioning and residual-aware weighting, and generalization from independent snapshots to joint space-time reconstruction. 8Datasets and tasks. For spatial reconstruction, we consider Navier–Stokes vorticity fields at Reynolds number Re = 10 ,000 (NS2D; 1024 ×1024 ) and boiling-process fields from BubbleML (Hassan et al., 2023) (PoolBoiling2D; 384 × 384 ). The former contain multiscale vortices and thin shear layers, while the latter contain sparse localized interfaces in relatively smooth backgrounds. We uniformly select five temporal snapshots from each simulation trajectory and fit each snapshot independently. For joint spatiotemporal reconstruction, we train a single INR over continuous (x, y, t ) coordinates spanning 64 consecutive frames: PoolBoiling3D contains 384 × 384 × 64 points, and NS3D contains 

512 × 512 × 64 points. Here, the third coordinate is time. 

Sampling baselines. We compare ACES with uniform random sampling, Implicit Neural Teaching (INT) (Zhang et al., 2024), EVOlutionary Selector (EVOS) (Zhang et al., 2025), and Fixed Stratified ,which uses a static uniform grid with a matched stratified allocation to isolate the benefit of adaptive partitioning. In the joint spatiotemporal experiments, EVOS is omitted because the evaluated implementation is not directly applicable to this setting. 

Evaluation protocol. Our main experiments use a SIREN (Sitzmann et al., 2020) backbone and SGD. The spatial experiments sample a fraction 2 × 10 −3 of the candidate points per iteration, while the larger joint spatiotemporal experiments use 7 × 10 −4. Learning rates are selected by a coarse search for each dataset and method. Because each task reconstructs a known field, training draws coordinates from that field and evaluation computes relative mean squared error (relative MSE) over the full coordinate grid; no conventional train/test split is used. Relative MSE is normalized by the squared norm of the ground-truth field. Results are averaged over three random seeds, and tables report mean ± standard deviation. Appendix F evaluates an additional Fourier Feature Network backbone; further implementation and training details are provided in Appendix G.

4.2 Ablation Study 

We perform ablation studies on the PoolBoiling2D dataset to evaluate the contributions of adaptive partitioning and region-level weighting through three variants: (1) Fixed + Weighting, which applies residual-aware region weighting on top of the fixed partition, (2) Partition Only (Unbiased), which uses adaptive partitioning without residual-aware weighting, and (3) Full ACES, which combines adaptive partitioning with residual-aware weighting. Figure 4 shows that both adaptive partitioning and residual-aware weighting contribute to the final performance. Adaptive partitioning alone already improves convergence over Fixed + Weighting, indicating that structured coverage and reduced sampling redundancy are important for efficient optimization. Adding residual-aware weighting further improves convergence speed, suggesting that adaptive partitioning and focused weighting provide complementary benefits. Appendix H additionally studies sensitivity to the weighting exponent α and partition-update interval.

# 5 Conclusion 

We introduced ACES, a region-level adaptive sampling framework for efficient training of implicit neural representations on heterogeneous continuous fields. ACES combines adaptive spatial par-titioning with residual-aware weighting to balance domain coverage and focused refinement. Our analysis shows that adaptive partitioning reduces stochastic gradient variance through increased within-region homogeneity, while biased region-level weighting can improve optimization efficiency. Experiments on scientific field learning tasks demonstrate faster convergence and improved sample efficiency over uniform and pointwise adaptive sampling baselines, particularly in settings with highly localized complexity. The current implementation incurs additional sampling overhead, and the analysis characterizes local one-step behavior rather than long-term nonconvex dynamics. These results suggest that structured region-level sampling is a promising direction for efficient neural field 12 learning.

# F Generalization to a Fourier Feature Network 

To test whether the benefits of ACES depend on the SIREN architecture used in the main experiments, we replace SIREN with a Fourier Feature Network (FFN) (Tancik et al., 2020) and repeat the PoolBoiling2D comparison. All sampling methods use the same FFN backbone and evaluation protocol. Table 2 reports relative MSE throughout training, averaged over three random seeds. Uniform sampling and INT obtain lower mean error during the earliest checkpoints, whereas ACES obtains the lowest mean error from 800 iterations onward. At 5,000 iterations, ACES reaches a relative MSE of 0.1536 ± 0.0263 , compared with 0.2866 ± 0.0411 for uniform sampling. # H Runtime and Hyperparameter Sensitivity 

H.1 Runtime and Overhead Analysis 

We re-profile the current NS2D configuration on a single NVIDIA A100-SXM4-80GB GPU. For each of three random seeds, we first run 20 warm-up iterations and then measure 1,000 training iterations. We synchronize CUDA before and after every timed region and separately measure the model-training stage, ordinary sampling, and adaptive partition construction. The model-training stage includes the weighted loss, forward and backward passes, gradient processing, and optimizer update. ACES rebuilds its partition every 200 iterations; therefore, each measured run contains five partition updates. Table 3 reports the mean and standard deviation across the three runs. ACES costs approximately 1.80 × as much per iteration as uniform sampling. Most of the difference comes from region-wise sample allocation and construction of the weighted minibatch; periodic partition construction contributes only 0.161 ms per iteration after amortization. The larger training-stage cost reflects the weighted loss operations. 

H.2 Sensitivity to Weighting and Partition Updates 

We first vary the residual weighting exponent α while keeping the remaining sampler settings fixed. Table 4 reports relative MSE averaged over five frames. Increasing α from a weak weighting regime improves reconstruction on both datasets, while the improvement saturates in a moderate range.

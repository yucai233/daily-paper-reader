Abstract —We present ChromaGS, a method for real-time, language-guided color editing of animatable 3D Gaussian head avatars. Given a trained animatable avatar, users can instantly modify the color of semantic regions through natural language, with edits applied at render time and no retraining required. Our key insight is to augment each Gaussian primitive with learned soft assignments to semantic regions and decompose colors into region-level base colors and Gaussian-level residuals. This decomposition enables coherent color transfer: modifying a region’s base color propagates naturally through all associated Gaussians while preserving fine appearance details encoded in residuals. A two-stage language pipeline translates text instruc-tions into target colors, supporting both absolute specifications and relative adjustments. Unlike generative editing methods that may introduce unintended modifications, our approach provides deterministic, precisely localized semantic control. Experiments demonstrate faithful appearance preservation and intuitive inter-action across diverse subjects. Project page and code are available at: https://a-canela.github.io/chromags/ 

Index Terms —3D Gaussian splatting, animatable head avatars, semantic editing, language-guided editing 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Photorealistic 3D head avatars have become essential for applications ranging from telepresence and virtual reality to film production and gaming. Recent advances in neural radiance fields [1] and 3D Gaussian splatting [2] have enabled high-fidelity avatar reconstruction from monocular video, with animatable variants supporting real-time expression-driven rendering [3], [4]. However, editing the appearance of these avatars remains challenging. Users often want to modify attributes-eye color, skin tone, hair color-for creative customization or virtual try-on, without altering identity. Existing generative editing meth-ods based on diffusion models, while powerful, often introduce unintended modifications, struggle with precise localization, and cannot guarantee deterministic outputs [5]. We present ChromaGS, enabling real-time, language-guided semantic color editing of 3D Gaussian head avatars (Fig. 3), where users specify modifications through natural language and see results instantly. Our key idea is to augment Gaussian primitives with seman-tic awareness and decompose their colors in a way that enables coherent region-level editing while preserving fine details. A language interface translates text instructions into target colors, supporting both absolute and relative modifications. Our contributions are: (1) a semantic Gaussian representa-tion with learned soft region assignments enabling localized color editing; (2) a color decomposition that separates base colors from residual details; and (3) a two-stage language pipeline for robust, intuitive color specification. Experiments demonstrate instant, deterministic color editing with faithful appearance preservation across diverse subjects. II. R ELATED WORK 

Text-Guided 3D Gaussian Editing. Several methods address text-guided editing of 3D Gaussian scenes. GaussianEditor [6], Instruct-GS2GS [7], and 3DSceneEditor [8] leverage diffusion priors or open-vocabulary grounding for general scene editing, while LangSplat [9] embeds CLIP features into Gaussians for open-vocabulary queries. For head avatars, HeadStudio [10], HeadArtist [11], and HeadSculpt [12] generate stylized heads from text via score distillation, and GaussianAvatar-Editor [5] and FaceG2E [13] apply diffusion-based optimization for text-guided appearance changes. These generative approaches require per-edit optimization, may introduce unintended mod-ifications, and cannot guarantee deterministic outputs. Palette-Based 3D Recoloring. A complementary line of work decomposes 3D scene appearance into a small set of palette colors that can be interactively edited. PaletteNeRF [14] and RecolorNeRF [15] introduced this idea for NeRF, representing each point’s color as a linear combination of learnable palette bases. PaletteGaussian [16] extends the palette paradigm to 3D Gaussian Splatting, supporting manual, image-driven, and text-driven palette edits. While effective for global and region-level recoloring of static scenes, these methods do not model animation, expression-dependent appearance, or semantic re-gions of a face, all of which are central to our setting. 

Semantic Editing in 3D Gaussian Scenes. Gaussian Group-ing [17] and OpenGaussian [18] assign semantic labels to Gaussians for segmentation-based scene editing. However, these methods target general static scenes and do not handle animatable avatars or language-driven color specification. Our approach combines learned semantic assignments with a color decomposition tailored for animatable head avatars, enabling region-specific editing that remains consistent across expres-sions and viewpoints. 

Semantic Segmentation and Language-Guided Editing. 

Vision-language models like SAM [19] and SAM3 [20] enable open-vocabulary segmentation via text prompts. For text-driven manipulation, diffusion-based approaches [21], [22] enable powerful but stochastic editing. We leverage SAM3 for semantic mask extraction and propose a deterministic language-guided color editing pipeline. III. M ETHOD 

A. Semantic Masks Extraction 

For each semantic class k and each training frame, we use SAM3 [20] with text prompts to generate binary masks Mk.Iris masks are obtained from eye masks by detecting ellipses and warping an annular-shaped template. Masks are made mu-tually exclusive by prioritizing smaller regions (e.g., iris over eye sclera) to ensure proper layering. SAM3 exhibits strong temporal consistency across frames due to its concept-level understanding; moreover, since our optimization aggregates supervision from hundreds of frames, occasional segmentation noise is naturally absorbed during training. 

B. Text-Guided Color Editing 

To enable intuitive color specification, we propose a two-stage architecture that decouples semantic parsing from color resolution, improving robustness by assigning simpler, well-defined tasks to each component. 

Semantic Parsing. We fine-tune Flan-T5-Small [23] to parse natural language instructions into a structured intermediate format. Given input text (e.g., “red matte lipstick”), the model outputs a structured string: region, color, type (e.g., lips, red matte, absolute ). Multiple edits are separated by semicolons. This simplified output space (region names, color descriptors, and edit types) is significantly easier to learn than direct RGB prediction. 

Color Resolving. The structured output is processed by a deterministic color resolver that handles two types of instruc-tions: 

Absolute. For absolute color instructions (e.g., “olive skin”), we encode the color description using a pretrained sentence encoder (all-MiniLM-L6-v2) [24], [25] and find the closest match via cosine similarity against pre-computed embeddings of region-specific palettes. These palettes contain anatomically plausible colors (e.g., Fitzpatrick scale [26] tones for skin, natural iris colors based on melanin distributions) and serve as the target color space for each region. 

Relative. For relative instructions (e.g., “darker”, “more sat-urated”), we convert the current region color to HSV and apply deterministic modifiers (e.g., reducing V for “darker”, increasing S for “more saturated”).

Implementation Details. We implement ChromaGS on top of an animatable Gaussian splatting framework [4]. We define 

K = 6 semantic classes (hair, eyebrows, eyes, lips, skin, plus background). Training runs for 50k iterations on a single NVIDIA RTX 5090, taking approximately 15 minutes. 

Datasets and Evaluation Setup. We evaluate on scenes from FaceScape [28] and INSTA [29], featuring diverse skin tones, eye colors, and hair styles. Each scene is edited with 14 diverse color edits spanning hair, eyes, skin, lips, and eyebrows, yield-ing approximately 10k edited frames per method at 512 × 512 

resolution. 

Metrics. We measure: (1) Identity Preservation ↑: Arc-Face cosine similarity between edited and unedited renders; (2) CLIP Score ↑: alignment between rendered image and target text prompt, averaged across three CLIP backbones (ViT-L/14, ViT-B/16, ViT-B/32); (3) Context Preservation ↑:computed as 1 − LPIPS outside the target region, measuring how well non-target areas are preserved after editing. A. Comparison with Baselines 

We compare against GaussianAvatar-Editor [5], a text-guided Gaussian head avatar editing method using diffusion priors, and InstructPix2Pix [22], an image-conditioned dif-fusion model that applies text-guided edits frame-by-frame. While other text-guided 3D editing methods exist, they target static scenes rather than animatable avatars [6], [17], [7], [8], [16] or edit generated heads instead of reconstructions from real video [10], [11], [12], [13], making a direct comparison neither applicable nor fair; the selected baselines represent the closest available approaches to our setting. Fig. 5 shows color editing results across multiple subjects and semantic regions. Our method produces consistent, localized color changes that preserve identity and fine details (e.g., specular highlights, texture variations). Edits propagate naturally across different expressions and viewpoints due to the 3D Gaussian represen-tation. Table II presents quantitative comparisons using the pre-trained scenes publicly released by GaussianAvatar-Editor [5]. Our method achieves substantially higher identity and con-text preservation scores, reflecting its ability to confine edits precisely to the target region. The baselines obtain slightly higher CLIP scores despite producing visibly less accurate edits (Fig. 5). This apparent paradox arises because CLIP mea-sures holistic image-text similarity: a diffusion-based method that tints the entire face green when asked for “green eyes” produces a stronger global color signal that CLIP rewards, even though the edit is spatially incorrect. Our method, by design, restricts color changes to the semantically correct region, yielding a subtler global shift that CLIP is less sensitive to, a trade-off we consider desirable. Beyond metric performance, the methods differ substantially in practical usability (Table I). GaussianAvatar-Editor couples editing and training into a single optimization loop of ∼800 s, which must be repeated for every new edit. Our method shares a comparable one-time training cost, but decouples editing entirely: once trained, any color change is applied in 

∼50 ms (which mainly corresponds to the language model inference mapping the text prompt to color parameters), three orders of magnitude faster, enabling interactive exploration of diverse edits. InstructPix2Pix operates frame-by-frame with no 3D awareness, which not only limits throughput but also introduces severe temporal flickering across the sequence. 

B. Ablation Studies 

Semantic Supervision. Table III and Fig. 6 show that omitting semantic supervision ( λsem = 0) prevents Gaussians from learning distinct region assignments, causing edits to erro-neously affect unrelated facial regions. This confirms Lsem 

is essential for regularizing primitives into coherent semantic clusters, effectively anchoring color modulation to the intended targets through robust semantic disentanglement. V. L IMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK

Our approach enables uniform shifts but precludes texture/material modifications (e.g., metallic skin, freckles) and geometric edits (e.g., reshaping features). VI. C ONCLUSION 

We introduced ChromaGS for real-time, language-driven color editing of animatable 3D Gaussian avatars. By coupling learned segmentation with color decomposition, our frame-work enables localized, deterministic control without requir-ing per-edit optimization. Our two-stage pipeline effectively bridges natural language with precise absolute and relative color adjustments. Ultimately, replacing diffusion-based op-timization with a single forward pass allows ChromaGS to make high-fidelity avatar customization practical for interac-tive gaming and virtual try-on. VII.

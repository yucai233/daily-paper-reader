ABSTRACT 

The C IV 𝜆𝜆 1548 , 1551 resonance doublet is a key tracer of warm gas ( 𝑇 ∼ 10 5 K) within and around galaxies. Recent observations have detected this line in both absorption and emission, revealing asymmetric profiles in galaxies and spatially extended haloes around active galactic nuclei (AGNs). Resonance scattering can strongly modify the emergent spectra and spatial distributions, complicating their interpretation. Using 3D Monte Carlo radiative transfer simulations, we study C IV resonance scattering over a broad range of column densities, intrinsic emission-line widths, and outflow velocities. We find that multiple scattering broadens the line profile and, in outflowing media, modifies the doublet ratio, 𝑅 CIV , defined as the flux ratio of the K and H components at 1548 and 1551 Å, respectively. When the outflow velocity approaches or exceeds the doublet separation (≃ 500 km s −1), K-line photons are redistributed around the H component, driving 𝑅 CIV below its intrinsic value and, in optically thick fast outflows, even below unity. We also combine photoionization models with resonance scattering to investigate extended C IV haloes around AGNs and compare them with He II 𝜆 1640 emission. Simple photoionization models do not produce C IV emission more extended than He II, whereas resonance scattering redistributes locally produced and central-source C IV photons to larger radii. These results demonstrate that the C IV doublet ratio and spatial distribution provide complementary diagnostics of warm gas. 

Key words: radiative transfer – scattering – line: profiles – galaxies: active – galaxies: haloes 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Galactic winds driven by star formation and active galactic nuclei (AGNs) are ubiquitously observed across cosmic time and exhibit multiphase structure extending over several kpc (e.g., Veilleux et al. 2005). These outflows play a crucial role in galaxy evolution by mediating interactions between the host galaxy and its circumgalac-tic medium (CGM), the vast gas reservoir within the host-galaxy halo. They eject fuel for star formation into the CGM or intergalactic medium, regulate the accretion of gas from the CGM into the inter-stellar medium, and enrich the accreted material (Faucher-Giguère & Oh 2023; Thompson & Heckman 2024). Resonance doublet lines are powerful diagnostics of galactic winds and the CGM. The different ionization energies of these lines have been used to investigate the multiphase structure of gas in galactic outflows and the CGM (e.g., Steidel et al. 2010; Heckman et al. 2017). Ly 𝛼 and low-ionization atoms such as Mg II ( ∼ 15 .0 eV) trace cold gas ( 𝑇 ∼ 10 4 K), and high-ionization atoms such as C IV , N V, and O VI ( ≥ 47 .9 eV) trace warm and hot gas ( 𝑇 ≥ 10 5 K). In particular, the C IV resonance doublet at 𝜆𝜆 1548 , 1551 is a key tracer of warm gas at 𝑇 ≈ 10 5 K, where C IV fraction peaks. As a result, C IV serves as an important coolant and tracer of gas at 𝑇 ∼ 10 5 K(e.g., Tumlinson et al. 2017).

The C IV resonance doublet has been studied both in absorption and emission. From absorption line studies, C IV has been reported with typical column densities of 𝑁 CIV = 10 13 −15 cm −2 (e.g., Codor-eanu et al. 2018; Rudie et al. 2019; Davies et al. 2023), in reason-able agreement with cosmological simulations (Garza et al. 2024), and it has been observed out to impact parameters of ∼ 100 kpc (e.g., Bordoloi et al. 2014; Liang & Chen 2014; Dutta et al. 2021). Strong C IV emission has been detected in sources ranging from stel-lar objects (e.g., Feibelman 1983; Michalitsianos et al. 1988, 1992; Thanathibodee et al. 2024) to nebulae around star-forming galaxies and AGNs (e.g., Heckman et al. 1991; Arrigoni Battaia et al. 2015a,b; Stark et al. 2015; Berg et al. 2019a; Travascio et al. 2020; Guo et al. 2020; Fossati et al. 2021; Matthee et al. 2022; Topping et al. 2024). Through the detection of C IV emission, the spatial distribution and kinematics of diffuse warm gas can be investigated without the need for bright background sources. The detection of C IV emission raises an interesting question about the doublet ratio of C IV 𝜆 1548 and 𝜆 1551 ( 𝐹 1548 /𝐹 1551 ). In par-ticular, highly ionized star-forming galaxies exhibit strong nebular C IV emission (e.g., Berg et al. 2019b; Izotov et al. 2024), with the two doublet components clearly resolved, making them ideal targets for measuring the doublet ratio. Theoretically, the intrinsic doublet ra-tio is 2 due to their statistical weights of the transitions. However, the observed C IV spectra reveal variations in doublet ratios, with a wide range of values from 0.3 to 2.4 (e.g., Stark et al. 2015; Vanzella et al. 

© 2026 The Authors arXiv:2609.11487v1 [astro-ph.GA] 10 Sep 2026 2 Lim et al. 

2016; Senchyna et al. 2017; Tang et al. 2024). For instance, Topping et al. (2025) reported a ratio below unity ( 𝐹 1548 /𝐹 1551 = 0.8), where the spectrum is characterized by a strong C IV 𝜆 1551 and a weak 

𝜆 1548. This is different from the intrinsic profile of the C IV doublet emission. The authors presumed that radiative transfer processes may modify the intrinsic doublet ratio during photon escape. Recently, C IV emission has also been detected as extended nebu-lae out to several tens of kpc around AGNs with extended He II 𝜆 1640 nebulae (e.g., Guo et al. 2020; Fossati et al. 2021; Sabhlok et al. 2024; Galbiati et al. 2026). Given the similar high-ionization energies of C IV and He II (47.9 and 54.4 eV, respectively), these lines are often detected together in the warm CGM at 𝑇 ≈ 10 5 K. Observations also show that C IV emission is usually brighter and more spatially extended than He II at current surface-brightness limits. For exam-ple, Galbiati et al. (2026) detected extended C IV and He II nebulae around a 𝑧 = 3.66 quasar with sizes of ≈ 55 and ≈ 45 kpc and C IV /He II ratios well above unity. In addition, Travascio et al. (2020) reported extended C IV emission out to ∼ 75 kpc in the absence of He II emission. To generate these high-ionization emission lines, a hard radiation field is required so that photoionization by a powerful central source has traditionally been considered the primary mecha-nism. However, several studies have shown that pure photoionization models fail to reproduce the observations unless crude approxima-tions of resonance scattering are taken into account (Fossati et al. 2021; Obreja et al. 2024). Given the fundamental differences in the radiative properties of C IV and He II , the observed C IV halo suggests that pure photoionization models struggle to explain these results, and that additional mechanisms are required. Resonance scattering in outflowing gas is frequently invoked as main mechanism to explain the observed C IV emission from star-forming galaxies and AGN. Given that the C IV 𝜆𝜆 1548 , 1551 dou-blet arises from a resonance transition, C IV photons can undergo multiple scatterings before escaping. In addition, C IV is produced predominantly through collisional excitation, with a minor contribu-tion from recombination, in warm ionized gas, making it a sensitive tracer of such outflows. Indeed, powerful galactic winds, with veloc-ities up to ∼ 1000 km s −1, are commonly observed in star-forming galaxies and AGN. However, current studies of radiative transfer of C IV are insufficient to fully understand its spatial distribution and spectral variations. In this paper, we use a 3D Monte Carlo radiative transfer (RT) code, RT-scat (Chang et al. 2023; Chang & Gronke 2024), to study C IV RT as a tracer of warm gas at 𝑇 ≈ 10 5 K. This paper is organized as follows. In § 2, we introduce the physics of radiative transfer and describe the details of our 3D Monte Carlo simulations for a spherical scattering medium surrounding a point emission source. In § 3, we present the simulated spectral profiles of C IV emission for both a monochromatic source and a Gaussian source, and investigate the doublet ratio of C IV 𝜆 1548 and 𝜆 1551, and the spatial distribution for the case of a Gaussian source in an outflowing medium. In § 4, we discuss the results of our C IV radiative transfer simulations. Finally, § 5 summarizes our main conclusions.

2.4 Monte Carlo Radiative Transfer Simulations 

Our Monte Carlo simulation is based on the methodology presented in Section 2.5 of Chang & Gronke (2024) and explores the scattering behavior of C IV doublet, following five steps: (i) The scattering gas geometry and kinematics are characterized by the physical parameters listed in Table 1. The physical properties of each cell in a 3D Cartesian grid, with 200 cells per dimension, are assigned based on the position of its center. (ii) We generate a total of 10 7 photons from the central source, assigning each photon a wavelength 𝜆 𝑖 drawn from a Gaussian profile with width 𝜎 int and an isotropically distributed propagation direction 

k𝑖 .(iii) The optical depth to the next scattering event is drawn as 

𝜏 𝑓 = − ln 𝑟 𝑢 , where 𝑟 𝑢 is a uniform random number between 0 and 1. Using the scattering cross section 𝜎 𝜈 defined in Equation 1, the corresponding physical free path is then obtained as 𝑙 𝑓 = 𝜏 𝑓 /𝑛 CIV 𝜎 𝜈 ,where 𝑛 CIV is the number density of C IV ions, 𝑛 CIV = 𝑁 CIV /Δ𝑅 .This free path determines the location of the next scattering event. (iv) After each scattering event, the direction of the scattered photon k𝑠 and its wavelength 𝜆 𝑠 are determined by the scattering phase function and the frequency redistribution function, respec-tively. Since Ly 𝛼 and C IV have similar resonance scattering struc-tures, we adopt the Ly 𝛼 scattering phase function and frequency re-distribution function (Chang et al. 2023), following the methodology of Chang & Gronke (2024) for the Mg II resonance doublet. (v) Steps (iii)-(iv) are repeated until the photon escapes the whole grid. Once it escapes, 𝜆 𝑠 is converted to the observed-frame wave-length, and the escaping photon is collected for the spectrum and surface brightness. The calculation then returns to Step (ii) to gener-ate and propagate the next photon. The Monte Carlo simulation ends once the number of generated photons reaches the specified target total number. In this study, we neglect polarization or dust effects 2 in the C IV resonance lines, although RT-scat is capable of modeling both effects, as demonstrated in a previous Mg II study (Chang & Gronke 2024). 

3 RESULT 

In this section, we present the results of our C IV radiative transfer modeling. Section 3.1 describes the formation of the C IV spectrum in a static medium with a monochromatic source. Section 3.2 examines the C IV spectrum for a Gaussian source in two cases: (i) a static medium and (ii) an outflowing medium. For the outflowing medium case, we also explore the C IV doublet ratio as a diagnostic of fast outflows. In Section 3.3, we investigate the spatial distribution of C IV emission, including photoionization and scattering effects. 

3.1 Line formation of C IV–Monochromatic source 

To study the fundamental effects of radiative transfer on the C IV doublet, we begin with idealized conditions: a static medium (𝑉 out = 0 km s −1) and a monochromatic source ( 𝜎 int = 0 km s −1). Given that 𝜏 0 is jointly determined by 𝑁 CIV and 𝜎 R (see Equation 6), the optical depth at the line center plays a key role in shaping the line profile. As described in Section 2.2, scattered photons escape directly at the line center in the optically thin regime ( 𝜏 0 < 1), whereas in the optically thick regime ( 𝜏 0 > 1), they are trapped near the line center and undergo multiple scatterings. This indicates that multiple scattering is the primary mechanism driving the variation of the C IV spectrum. In the following section, we investigate the impact of multiple scattering on line broadening.

3.1.2 Numerical solution compared with analytic solution 

As discussed in Section 3.1.1, the emergent line profile primarily depends on the optical depth. To quantify the effects of multiple scattering, we measure the peak separation Δ𝑉 peaks and the mean number of scatterings ⟨𝑁 scat ⟩ of the C IV K line in our RT simulations and compare them with analytic estimates (e.g., Osterbrock 1962; Adams 1972; Neufeld 1990). To derive analytical estimates for Δ𝑉 peaks and ⟨𝑁 scat ⟩, we first de-fine the threshold frequency beyond which photons can escape the medium after diffusing in frequency space through multiple scatter-ings. Spectral peaks naturally emerge near the frequency at which the medium becomes optically thin. We designate this as the escape frequency, 𝜈 esc , with its corresponding dimensionless frequency pa-rameter denoted as 𝑥 esc . Photons satisfying |𝑥 | > 𝑥 esc can escape the system either directly or after undergoing only ≈ 1–2 scatterings. Photons diffuse simultaneously in both real space and frequency space, as illustrated in § 2.4, while in moderately optically thick me-dia (1 ≲ 𝜏 0 ≲ 10 4), the frequency-space diffusion remains within the Gaussian core regime (Draine 2011). Just prior to their last scattering event, scattered photons reach a frequency parameter of 

𝑥 ≈ 𝑥 esc and establish a characteristic spatial distribution within the sphere. We do not explicitly model the spatial distribution of the last-scattering points. However, by definition, at the location of the last scattering, the medium is effectively optically thin to photons with the escape frequency parameter 𝑥 ≈ 𝑥 esc , even if the system remains optically thick to line-center photons ( 𝑥 = 0). 3.2 The formation of the C IV spectrum–Gaussian Source 

In this section, we explore the formation of the C IV doublet for a Gaussian source ( 𝜎 int > 0 km s −1) as a function of the intrinsic line width, 𝜎 int , for two types of scattering media: static and outflowing media. We adopt a fixed velocity dispersion 𝜎 R = 11 .8 km s −1,corresponding to the thermal speed at 𝑇 = 10 5 K. In addition, since the velocity separation between the K and H components is Δ𝑉 K,H ≃

500 km s −1, we define the K and H line regions as the velocity ranges within Δ𝑉 = 250 km s −1 from each line center. 

3.2.1 C IV spectra from a static medium 

As shown in § 2.3, we assume a broad range of an intrinsic width 

𝜎 int from 0 to 3000 km s −1 (cf. Table 1). Unlike a monochromatic source ( 𝜎 int = 0 km s −1), the doublet components of a Gaussian source can be intrinsically blended when 𝜎 int is sufficiently broad relative to their separation, Δ𝑉 K,H ≃ 500 km s −1, even in the ab-sence of radiative transfer effects. To isolate this intrinsic effect, we examine the C IV doublet spectra in a static, optically thin medium. Figure 7 shows C IV doublet spectra for 𝜎 int = 50, 100, 200, 500, 1000, and 3000 km s −1. For narrow intrinsic widths (i.e., 

𝜎 int ≤ 100 km s −1), the K and H components are clearly resolved, as is commonly observed in the spectra of stars and star-forming galaxies. As 𝜎 int increases beyond 100 km s −1, the two lines begin to overlap. When 𝜎 int ≳ Δ𝑉 K,H ≃ 500 km s −1, the two components be-come fully blended, making the doublet structure indistinguishable. This motivates our choice of 𝜎 int = 500 km s −1 as a representative AGN case in the subsequent simulations, even though observed AGN typically exhibit larger intrinsic widths. 

3.2.2 C IV spectra from an outflowing medium 

We now discuss how resonance scattering in an outflowing medium modifies the intrinsically broad, or even blended, C IV spectrum. Figures 8 and 9 show the C IV spectra for two Gaussian sources in an outflowing medium, one with clearly separated K- and H-lines (𝜎 int = 100 km s −1) and the other with fully blended lines ( 𝜎 int =500 km s −1), as functions of 𝑁 CIV and 𝑉 out . This occurs because outflows redistribute photons from the blue side to the red side through scattering. The right panel of Figure 8 presents the C IV spectra for 𝑉 out =

3.3 Spatially extended C IV halos around quasars 

Spatially extended nebulae of C IV 𝜆𝜆 1548, 1551 and He II 𝜆 1640 have been observed around quasars at 𝑧 > 2 (Guo et al. 2020; Travas-cio et al. 2020; Fossati et al. 2021; Sabhlok et al. 2024; Galbiati et al. 2026). Because C IV and He II have similar ionization energies, 47.9 and 54.4 eV, respectively, both lines are expected to trace warm, highly ionized gas in the CGM at 𝑇 ∼ 10 5 K. However, their radia-tive properties are fundamentally different. He II 𝜆 1640, the Balmer-

𝛼 transition of He II, is a non-resonance line that traces the lo-cal recombination emissivity of He III, whereas the C IV resonance doublet is predominantly produced by collisional excitation and can additionally undergo resonance scattering by C IV atom. As a re-sult, the spatial distribution of C IV emission does not necessarily need to follow that of He II . C IV photons can be redistributed to larger radii by scattering, while He II photons escape without reso-nance scattering. This distinction is important because photoioniza-tion models alone often struggle to reproduce the observed C IV and He II luminosities and their relative spatial distributions unless crude approximations of resonance scattering are taken into account. For example, Fossati et al. (2021) detected extended C IV emission out to ≃ 50 kpc in a stack of 27 bright quasars at 𝑧 = 3–4 .5, with a ten-tative detection of He II out to ≃ 40 kpc, but found that the observed spatial extents and line ratios are difficult to reproduce with pure photoionization models and require the addition of photon pump-ing. A similar result was also shown by Obreja et al. (2024) when post-processing a cosmological simulation of a massive halo ex-pected to host a 𝑧 ∼ 3 quasar. Only including the approximated treatment of broad-line region photon scattering allowed the authors to simultaneously match the observed levels of extended He II and C IV emission around quasars. 4 DISCUSSION 4.1 C IV doublet ratio as a tracer of fast outflow 

In § 3.2, we show that the doublet ratio exhibits significant variation when the outflow velocity is faster than the velocity separation of the doublet (see also Yoo et al. 2002; Chang & Gronke 2024). Even though the C IV doublet ratio 𝑅 CIV depends on the intrinsic width, a ratio less than unity is clear evidence of strong outflow as shown in Figure 10. This small doublet ratio has been observed in various stellar objects (Feibelman 1983; Michalitsianos et al. 1988, 1992; Cauley & Johns-Krull 2016), as well as in the strong, saturated P-Cygni profiles in OB stars and Wolf-Rayet stars (Willis et al. 1986; Crowther 2022), where a strong outflow at 𝑇 ∼ 10 5 K is able to exist. Furthermore, recent C IV observations of star-forming galaxies show a wide range of 𝑅 CIV values from 0.3 to 2.4 (e.g., Stark et al. 2015; Vanzella et al. 2016; Senchyna et al. 2017; Berg et al. 2019a,b; Matthee et al. 2022; Tang et al. 2024; Izotov et al. 2024; Witstok et al. 2021, 2025). Moreover, Berg et al. (2019b) showed that the C IV profile is broader than those of He II and O III ] emission lines, which are non-resonance lines with similar ionization energies. Thus, 

𝑅 CIV can serve as a tracer of warm gas outflows in star-forming galaxies through radiative transfer analysis. 4.2 C IV surface brightness & outflow 

Resonance scattering plays a crucial role in producing the extended C IV surface brightness around AGNs compared to the non-resonant He II 𝜆 1640 emission, as shown in Section 3.3. The efficiency of scattering is governed by both the kinematics and the physical prop-erties of the scattering medium. In particular, an outflowing, optically thick medium can expose photons to a broad range of line-of-sight velocities when the radial velocity is non-uniform. This effect also allows intrinsically broad emission of resonance lines, such as Ly 𝛼 

and Mg II, to scatter over a wider frequency range (e.g., Zheng & Miralda-Escudé 2002; Verhamme et al. 2006; Chang & Gronke 2024). This is a similar behavior to C IV as shown in Figures 8 and 9. The scattered photons in the broad frequency range contribute to the spatial expansion of Ly 𝛼 nebulae (Chang et al. 2023). Observa-tionally, AGN-driven outflows have also been observed in the CGM in association with extended C IV and He II nebulae (e.g., Sabhlok et al. 2024), and Rueda-Vargas et al. (2026) found strong correla-tions between the ISM-scale outflow velocity and both the size and luminosity of the surrounding Ly 𝛼 nebulae in five QSOs at 𝑧 ∼ 2–3, arguing that similar trends might be expected on CGM scales. Mo-tivated by these results, we explore in this section how the outflow velocity affects the C IV surface-brightness profiles, using models in Section 3.3. Figure 13 shows the C IV surface-brightness profiles of the ionization-bounded and density-bounded cases for outflow veloci-ties 𝑉 out = 0 − 3000 km s −1. The resonantly scattered C IV emission in the outflowing medium is brighter than that in the static case (𝑉 out = 0 km s −1) out to the outskirts. However, the efficiency of scattering shows different behavior depending on the ionization con-dition. In the density-bounded case, the surface-brightness profile of C IV emission brightens with increasing 𝑉 out at most radii. The ionization-bounded case presents similar behavior within the ioniza-tion front ( 𝑟 p ≲ 30 kpc). At larger radii ( > 60 kpc), the profiles in outflowing cases are similar and clearly more extended than those in the static case. These results indicate that resonance scattering in outflow enhances the C IV surface brightness and contributes signif-icantly to spatial expansion. Our results are also directly relevant to observations of extended C IV and He II haloes around AGNs. At current surface brightness limits, He II nebulae are generally more compact and fainter than their C IV counterparts (Guo et al. 2020; Zhang et al. 2023; Sabhlok et al. 2024; Galbiati et al. 2026). In some cases, extended C IV emission is detected with no He II counterpart (Travascio et al. 2020). Res-onance scattering can account for this difference by redistributing C IV photons to larger radii, whereas He II directly traces the local emissivity. In Figure 13, the C IV profiles at 𝑉 out ≥ 300 km s −1 are much brighter than He II in the outer radius > 40 kpc, while the static case profile is comparable to the He II profile. These results show that resonance scattering in outflowing gas can be important for interpreting highly ionized nebular emission. More generally, the spatial expansion of resonance lines is de-termined by both the distribution and kinematics of the scattering gas. Velocity gradients systematically shift resonance photons in fre-quency and reduce trapping (Bonilha et al. 1979). Observationally, the similar distributions of [C II] and Ly 𝛼 haloes, along with the larger Ly 𝛼 velocity offsets associated with extended [C II] emission, connect the kinematics of cold gas to the spatial redistribution of Ly 𝛼 photons (Fujimoto et al. 2020). Our results extend this picture to warm ionized gas. At fixed gas distribution, outflow velocity extends the C IV scattering region. In real systems, however, outflows also change the amount and ionization state of the scattering gas. Costa et al. (2022) found that AGN feedback initially facilitates extended Ly 𝛼 emission by clearing dusty, H I gas from the nucleus, whereas stronger or sustained feedback lowers the H I column density in the halo and makes the Ly 𝛼 emission more compact. C IV may there-fore provide a complementary probe of warm outflows when Ly 𝛼 

scattering becomes less effective. 

4.3 Implications for C IV emission in Little Red Dots 

Recent James Webb Space Telescope ( JWST ) observations have re-vealed a population of compact sources in the early Universe, Little Red Dots (LRDs) with broad, asymmetric Balmer lines and V-shaped spectral energy distributions with blue UV and red optical contin-uum (Harikane et al. 2023; Übler et al. 2023; Kocevski et al. 2023; Maiolino et al. 2024; Matthee et al. 2024; Greene et al. 2024; Ko-cevski et al. 2025). Their compactness and broad emission lines suggest the presence of AGN, but many LRDs lack traditional AGN signatures, such as strong X-ray and radio emission or a prominent infrared excess (Sacchi & Bogdán 2025; Gloudemans et al. 2025; Xiao et al. 2025). This has motivated the idea that the radiative transfer effects of Balmer lines, such as Thomson and resonance scattering, produce broad, asymmetric Balmer lines without AGN properties (Rusakov et al. 2026; Chang et al. 2026; Sneppen et al. 2026). In other words, Thomson-thick gas (i.e., electron column density 𝑁 e > 10 24 cm −2) is required to generate broad wings around Balmer lines. C IV emission has now been detected in several LRDs (Labbe et al. 2024; Akins et al. 2025; Ando et al. 2026; Ji et al. 2026; Tang et al. 2026). Strong C IV accompanied by weak or undetected He II has often been interpreted as evidence for photoionization by low-metallicity massive stars rather than by a typical AGN spectrum. However, current observations do not uniquely determine whether C IV is produced by the compact LRD component or its host galaxy. Moreover, the ionizing source, the intrinsic emission region, and the gas through which the photons propagate are physically different. We show that the relative spatial distributions of C IV and He II can provide additional constraints in Section 4.2. 5 CONCLUSIONS 

This paper extended the 3D Monte Carlo radiative transfer code 

RT-scat (Chang & Gronke 2024) to include the C IV resonance doublet at 𝜆𝜆 1548 , 1551. Using a simple spherical geometry, we investigated the effects of C IV resonance scattering on the line for-mation, the variation of the doublet ratio, and the spatial distribution of C IV emission. Our main conclusions are summarized as follows: 

• Resonance scattering shapes the C IV spectrum. As the line-center optical depth 𝜏 0 increases, the profile evolves from a single Gaussian to a double-peaked shape with central suppression, broad-ening as FWHM /𝜎 R ≈ 1.092 + 1.061 √︃ ln 1 + ( 𝜏 0 /1.505 )2.788 . In an outflowing medium the doublet develops P-Cygni features, and once 𝑉 out exceeds the K–H separation ( Δ𝑉 K,H ≈ 500 km s −1), K-line photons are redistributed around the H component—naturally ex-plaining the observed dominance of the C IV 𝜆 1551 component in star-forming galaxies. 

• The doublet ratio 𝑅 CIV traces fast, warm outflows. 𝑅 CIV de-creases from its intrinsic value of ∼ 2 as 𝑁 CIV and 𝑉 out increase, falling below unity for strong outflows in the optically thick medium (see § 3.2.3 and Figure 12). Since intrinsic blending alone cannot drive 𝑅 CIV below unity, 𝑅 CIV < 1 can arise from resonance scatter-ing in strong outflows. Our 𝑁 CIV –𝑉 out map reproduces the range of 

𝑅 CIV observed in star-forming galaxies, demonstrating its diagnostic power on real data. 

• Scattering produces extended C IV halos. Photoionization alone cannot make C IV more extended than He II 𝜆 1640: the intrin-sic C IV profile is either more compact or much fainter than He II . Resonance scattering redistributes C IV photons—from in-situ pho-toionized gas, the broad-line region, and AGN continuum—to larger radii, producing extended C IV halos over several tens of kpc that exceed He II and brighten with 𝑉 out , comparable to the C IV halos observed around quasars (see Section 3.3 and Figure 13). Several open questions remain for future work. Other UV metal resonance doublets, such as O VI 𝜆𝜆 1032, 1038 and N V 

𝜆𝜆 1239, 1243, are expected to show similar behavior and, combined with C IV , can constrain the multiphase structure of the warm CGM at 𝑇 ≥ 10 5 K. More realistic geometries—including gas clumpiness, anisotropic optical depths, and polarization effects—may also intro-duce more complex behavior in the emergent doublet ratios (Seon 2024; Chang & Gronke 2024).
